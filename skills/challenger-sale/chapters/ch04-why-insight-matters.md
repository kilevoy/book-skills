# Chapter 4 — Teaching for Differentiation (Part 1): Why Insight Matters

## Core idea
Customers value sellers who help them think differently about their business, not only those who ask questions about needs they already know or present features. **Commercial teaching** is valuable when it reveals a real problem or opportunity and connects it to a distinctive capability the supplier can actually deliver.

## Why insight matters
The authors describe a customer-loyalty study in which sales experience accounted for 53% of the measured B2B loyalty drivers, while brand/product/service and price-value accounted for the rest. They report that customers valued sellers who brought a unique perspective, helped evaluate alternatives, advised them, prevented risk, and taught them something relevant. These are results reported in the book from SEC research, not current universal percentages.

Traditional discovery can uncover explicit needs, but buyers may not yet recognize the cost of a problem or the opportunity available. An insight-led conversation reframes the situation: it helps the customer see why a familiar practice is costly, risky, or limiting. The seller’s value is in the perspective and the useful next step, not simply in describing the product.

## Four tests for commercial teaching
1. **Lead to a unique supplier strength.** The insight must connect to a capability the company can credibly deliver better or differently. Do not create demand for a problem that the supplier cannot solve.
2. **Challenge an assumption.** Offer a new way to view the customer’s business, rather than confirming what they already believe. Agreement alone does not prove the customer learned something.
3. **Compel action.** Explain the business consequences of staying as-is and the value of addressing the issue. Quantify opportunity or risk where evidence supports it.
4. **Scale across a meaningful segment.** Build repeatable insights for customers with similar needs or behaviors. Do not rely on every seller inventing a new diagnosis from scratch.

## Practical use
Before creating a pitch, answer: “Why should this customer act, and why can our organization credibly help?” Replace generic claims—innovation, leadership, customer focus—with evidence and customer consequences. Then test whether the insight would still matter if the product name were removed; if not, the pitch may be a product presentation rather than commercial teaching.

## Limits
Customer insight must be grounded in reliable information and tailored to the actual account. The book’s claims are based on SEC studies from the period, not a substitute for current customer research. Commercial teaching is not permission to exaggerate risk or manufacture urgency.
