# Chapter 7 — Taking Control of the Sale

## Core idea
**Take Control** does not mean pressuring a buyer or reducing the sale to a last-minute price negotiation. It means holding a value-based position, challenging the customer’s thinking respectfully, and guiding a complex purchase toward a decision. The behavior is useful from the start: qualify the opportunity, help the customer understand how to buy, discuss money with confidence, and agree on specific next steps. Its credibility depends on the value established through teaching and tailoring.

## Qualify the opportunity and help the customer buy
The authors warn that some opportunities are **verification efforts**: the buyer has already selected a supplier but gathers competing proposals to validate the choice or negotiate with the incumbent. The book reports that close to 20% of opportunities in the research could fall into this category. Limited access—such as being allowed to meet only a junior contact while key decision makers remain unavailable—may be a signal. It is not proof. The seller should ask how the customer will decide, who needs to participate, and what role this evaluation plays before investing heavily in unpaid work.

A Challenger also helps a customer navigate the decision process. Buyers of complex solutions may not know who should be involved or what steps will be needed. Rather than passively ask the customer to define everything, the seller can propose a process based on relevant experience, request the right participants, and make the next decision clear. A related example addresses expensive presales work: before investing heavily in a solution, a supplier can be transparent about the effort and seek evidence of reciprocal commitment. The principle is to establish mutual seriousness early, not demand guarantees regardless of context.

## Assertiveness without aggression
The chapter separates three positions:
- **Passive:** yield to avoid discomfort, give concessions prematurely, or allow the customer to dictate all terms.
- **Aggressive:** attack, use antagonistic language, or impose the seller’s agenda without regard to the buyer.
- **Assertive:** state and defend a position clearly while listening, respecting the customer, and adapting to new information.

The authors relate passivity to perceived power imbalance, fear about the economy, doubts about the supplier’s value, and vague interpretations of “customer-centricity” as giving the buyer everything requested. They cite a BayGroup International survey in which 75% of sellers believed procurement held more power, while 75% of procurement respondents believed sellers did. This is a reported survey result, not a universal law. The practical lesson is to define customer orientation as creating durable value, not automatically discounting or accepting every short-term request.

In one example, a paint supplier seeks a price increase despite objections from procurement and threats to end the contract. The seller reinforces the value by showing increased productivity, enlists support from a production-site leader, and arranges a joint discussion with procurement. The buyer accepts the increase. The seller maintains a position without attacking the buyer.

## Plan negotiation: Situational Sales Negotiation (SSN)
The DuPont case uses **Situational Sales Negotiation (SSN)** from BayGroup International. Before negotiating, prepare the supplier’s strengths and weaknesses, information to seek or share, likely objections, target conditions, hypotheses about buyer needs, and the relative cost of each concession to the supplier and value to the customer. A discount can be expensive for the supplier but matter little to a buyer whose greater concern is performance or service. Preparation replaces an automatic, early concession with a deliberate exchange.

DuPont’s four-step conversation sequence is:
1. **Acknowledge and Defer:** recognize the concern and ask to explore value or needs before returning to price.
2. **Deepen and Broaden:** determine what the buyer seeks through a concession and expand the discussion to service, scope, timing, risk, or terms.
3. **Explore and Compare:** compare alternatives against the buyer’s outcomes, not just headline price. A different configuration may solve the underlying problem without a discount.
4. **Concede According to Plan:** if a concession is appropriate, use a planned sequence, begin with less costly terms, and seek an explicit reciprocal adjustment or commitment.

This is a framework for a constructive negotiation, not a way to evade a legitimate pricing concern. The form and order of concessions can affect whether the customer perceives a deal as fair.

## Keep momentum with a strong request
The authors’ **powerful request** connects a requested action, owner, and timing to an agreed customer outcome. In the server-infrastructure example, the seller ties a decision by a specific date to beginning installation in time to deliver a stated annual savings within the customer’s fiscal year. The connection should be real and agreed; it is not license to manufacture urgency. End meetings with a clear next step rather than a pleasant but directionless conversation.

## Application and limits
Before investing, understand the decision process, stakeholders, access, and buyer’s reason for evaluating alternatives. During resistance, hold the value position, ask what lies behind the objection, and compare options. Do not interpret every refusal as bad faith or use assertiveness to bully. Negotiation tactics must fit the contract, law, market, and relationship. The book’s percentages, survey, and examples are historical SEC/BayGroup material described in 2011; they are not current benchmarks or causal proof. The authors emphasize that value cannot be replaced by a confident tone.
