# Chapter 7 — Taking Control of the Sale

## Core idea
“Taking control” means guiding a complex purchase toward a clear, value-based decision while staying respectful and responsive. It is neither aggressive pressure nor simply negotiating at the end; it includes qualifying the opportunity, managing next steps, addressing resistance, and discussing money confidently.

## Diagnose before investing
Authors caution that some opportunities may be “tests” of an incumbent supplier rather than genuine competitive evaluations. Before investing heavily in presales work, understand the customer’s decision process, stakeholders, access, and reasons for evaluating alternatives. Do not assume every RFP represents an open contest.

## Assertive empathy
The chapter distinguishes passive behavior (giving ground to reduce tension), aggressive behavior (pushing without regard to the customer), and assertive behavior (holding a clear position while listening and adapting). Seller confidence should rest on a defensible business outcome, not on the belief that the product is inherently best. When the customer challenges price or value, explore the reason and bring the discussion back to the problem, desired outcome, and evidence.

## Negotiation planning and the four-step sequence
The DuPont example uses a pre-negotiation plan informed by Situational Sales Negotiation (SSN). Prepare strengths and weaknesses, questions, likely objections, target conditions, flexible terms, and the value of possible concessions. Evaluate concessions by both their cost to the seller and value to the buyer.

1. **Acknowledge and Defer:** recognize the request and ask permission to clarify needs and value before discussing a price change.
2. **Deepen and Broaden:** uncover why the buyer wants a concession and identify other dimensions—scope, service, timing, risk, or terms.
3. **Explore and Compare:** compare options by their actual business value, not only by headline price.
4. **Concede According to Plan:** if conceding, exchange it for a reciprocal commitment or adjustment; avoid unplanned discounts.

The sequence is a conversation framework, not a tactic for evading a legitimate concern. Use calibrated questions to understand the buyer’s objective and make the trade-off explicit.

## Keep momentum
End meetings with a concrete next action, owner, and timing. The book’s example of a “strong ask” connects a requested decision date to a customer outcome; make that connection only when it is real and agreed, not as manufactured urgency.

## Limits
Negotiation examples and the SSN method require adaptation to the contract, market, law, and customer relationship. Do not overstate value or use pressure disguised as empathy. The book is from 2011; treat its data as historical and seek current evidence for pricing and market claims.
