# Chapter 3 — The Challenger (Part 2): Exporting the Model to the Core

## Core idea
A Challenger is not simply a forceful seller who argues with customers. The profile combines **teach, tailor, take control**, delivered through **constructive tension**. The authors argue that organizations can help more sellers use these behaviors, but skill training alone is insufficient: the company must also create relevant insight, tools, manager coaching, and systems that reinforce the change. The model is an organizational capability and a long transformation, not a one-off course.

## Four implementation principles

### 1. Challengers are made, not just born
The authors say Challenger characteristics appeared to some degree across sellers, although the Challenger style was not the dominant mode for most. They therefore propose developing the ability to use Challenger behaviors in relevant customer conversations through tools, training, coaching, incentives, and recognition. The goal is not to replace nearly the whole team with a small elite. The authors acknowledge that not every seller will make the transition, but consider broad skill development more practical than wholesale replacement.

### 2. The combination of skills matters
The three skills reinforce one another. Teaching without tailoring can be irrelevant; tailoring without a new perspective can become a routine pitch; taking control without value is simply pressure. Individual components may be useful, but the full model depends on their integration. Constructive tension means inviting the customer to reconsider an assumption while remaining grounded in evidence and respect.

### 3. Challenging is an organizational capability
A company should not leave every seller to invent insights or make promises independently. Marketing and other central functions can develop repeatable **teaching pitches**: customer problems that matter and connect to capabilities the supplier can genuinely deliver. Sales adapts these messages to the customer’s industry, company, and stakeholders. The organization also needs research, business analytics, role-specific context, and feedback from field conversations. A seller’s role is not to recite a script, but to apply credible material flexibly and report what customers actually respond to.

### 4. Building a Challenger force is a journey
The early adopters described in the book saw the work as a transformation that takes years, not weeks. A training event without relevant messages, prepared managers, post-training coaching, and organizational reinforcement may create a brief lift but can be dismissed as another “flavor of the month.”

## The three capabilities in practice
**Teaching for Differentiation** means giving a useful new perspective on how the customer can compete, rather than leading with the supplier’s products. In the office-furniture example, a seller hears that the customer wants collaborative space and challenges the assumption that larger meeting rooms are always better. She uses data about group size and productivity, then connects the revised room design to a movable partition offered by her company. The insight shifts the discussion from the customer’s initial choice of a competitor to a problem the customer had not considered.

**Tailoring for Resonance** means linking the message to the outcomes and economic drivers that matter to the specific person. In the book’s outsourcing example, a company’s initial proposal focuses on cost savings. Before a meeting with the CEO, the sales team learns that his greater concern is customer satisfaction and that he is interested in technology. The team adjusts its presentation to show how the offering might both reduce cost and improve satisfaction and response speed, including real-time information for managers. The point is to translate one business insight into the audience’s own priorities, not merely change the slide title.

**Taking Control of the Sale** means keeping the conversation oriented toward value and a decision without becoming aggressive. In an example of a price increase, a buyer had previously requested expensive customized packaging. When the seller and customer rank what matters, that feature does not appear among the most important. They agree on a smaller price increase and standard packaging, improving the supplier’s profitability more than a price increase alone. The example illustrates that a firm position can be paired with listening and a change in scope.

## Organizational roles
Marketing can develop reusable insights, evidence, and audience-specific messages. Sales applies and tests them in conversations. Managers coach observable behavior and help sellers maintain focus on customer value. Senior leaders need to align training, incentives, tools, and expectations so that the model is supported rather than treated as an isolated initiative.

## Application and limits
Develop the three capabilities together and pair training with organizational support. Use real evidence, ensure that the supplier can deliver the proposed value, and adapt the conversation to the buyer. Do not equate constructive challenge with confrontation or tailoring with unsupported changes to the product promise. The examples and implementation claims are drawn from SEC research and early-adopter experience described in the 2011 book; they illustrate the authors’ approach but do not guarantee the same result in another company.
