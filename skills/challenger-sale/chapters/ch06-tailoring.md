# Chapter 6 — Tailoring for Resonance

## Core idea
As complex purchases involve more people, sellers must help each stakeholder see how a solution relates to the outcomes that matter in their role. Tailoring is not cosmetic personalization, a custom presentation for every person, or merely changing the vocabulary. It connects a sound business insight to the organization and to individual responsibilities, while creating the internal support needed for a decision.

## Buying group and the New Physics of Sales
The chapter distinguishes decision makers, who can approve or sign, from influencers and end users who affect evaluation and adoption. In the SEC research described in the book, the shared supplier experience mattered more to decision makers than isolated seller qualities; **widespread support across my organization** was a leading factor. Ease of buying also mattered, while price and customization were less prominent in the reported priorities. For influencers and end users, the seller’s professionalism, trustworthiness, useful perspective, and ability to teach were more important. These findings imply different work: decision makers need confidence that the supplier can work across the company; participants need useful, relevant interactions that help them advance their own goals.

The authors call the shift **The New Physics of Sales**. In the traditional pattern, the rep gathers information from participants and carries it upward to tailor a presentation for the executive. In the proposed pattern, the rep shares useful insight with participants; they help build and carry support toward the decision maker. This does not eliminate executive access. It means that access to a senior buyer is not a substitute for the internal consensus required in a complex sale.

## Layers of Tailoring
The authors’ map moves from broad context to the person:
1. **Industry:** relevant trends, market conditions, regulation, and competition.
2. **Company:** strategy, recent events, goals, and constraints.
3. **Role:** the results, metrics, and risks owned by the stakeholder.
4. **Individual:** that person’s priorities and concerns, validated in conversation.

Industry and company context can often be researched centrally. Role-level and individual-level assumptions need checking with the person; a job title is a hypothesis about priorities, not proof of what matters to this stakeholder.

## Customer outcomes and functional-bias cards
The chapter recommends describing **customer outcomes** concretely: the activity to improve, the measure used to judge performance, and the desired direction or amount of change. Examples include reducing defect rates by 5% on a production line or reducing the number of clicks needed for a website visitor to find an answer. The authors argue that role outcomes are often predictable, relatively stable, limited in number, and therefore scalable across accounts.

In the Solae case, a technical ingredient offering began reaching a wider set of stakeholders—marketing, manufacturing, procurement, and senior roles—while sellers continued to open with technical product attributes. That prompted “so what?” reactions from people whose work was not explained by the technical story. Solae created **functional-bias cards** for roles, capturing:
- business outcomes or criteria important to the role;
- **focus**: metrics and questions the person monitors regularly;
- **key concerns**: recurring practical worries; and
- **potential value areas**: levers the person can use to improve results.

The team then linked relevant solution capabilities to role-specific outcomes. The cards and scenarios are conversation aids, not scripts. A shared value plan records the project’s common objective, key stakeholders, desired result for each, likely concern, and a possible response. Solae discussed the plan with the customer, so the map reflected agreed needs rather than the seller’s assumptions.

## Application and limits
For a live deal, map participants, their roles, desired outcomes, concerns, and evidence that would matter to them. Give each participant insight they can use with colleagues instead of treating every contact as an information source for the seller. Systematize common role knowledge, but verify it in each account. Do not reduce people to job titles, treat a signed plan as proof of consensus, or bypass the buying group to reach the most senior person. The loyalty and stakeholder findings are SEC results reported in the 2011 book—not current universal ratios or proof that tailoring alone causes a sale.
